# ESD activity drafts — 15 November 2026

Status: approved for student testing on 16 November 2026; classroom feedback is pending. The starter targets six minutes; the longer activity targets 12–15 minutes. Neither timing is classroom-verified. Keep the existing set `hCAIK6CCo` unchanged.

Both use Harbour City's fictional waste partnership, serving the city, nearby towns and businesses. They are alternative planning exercises, not a sequence in which the first investment has already happened. Facilities operate at regional scale rather than serving one neighbourhood.

Costs are rounded historical benchmarks adjusted to a common 2024 price level. Emissions use a named historical treatment model. These are defensible teaching estimates, not current contractor quotations or measured results for Harbour City. Scenario quantities, records, budgets and targets are authored. ## Draft 1: Which two facilities? — six-minute starter

### Student introduction

Harbour City's waste partnership can fund **two facilities**. Work through the clues to decide which pair meets its budget and emissions target.

- **Glass:** cleans and sorts glass for new glass containers.
- **Plastics:** washes and processes rigid plastic for new plastic products.
- **Food:** turns separately collected food waste into biogas and fertiliser.
- **Garden:** composts separately collected garden waste for use in soil.

£1m means £1 million. CO₂e measures greenhouse gases using a common unit. Figures are rounded estimates; use the values provided.

### Stage 1 — Match the costs

The building costs are **£8m, £9m, £12m and £16m**. Each cost is used once.

1. Garden costs more than Plastics.
2. Glass costs more than Garden.
3. Food costs more than Glass.

Match each facility to its cost in a four-by-four logic grid. Optional hint: use the clues to arrange the facilities from cheapest to most expensive.

### Stage 2 — Apply the budget

The partnership has **£24m** for building. Select **every pair** it can afford.

- Glass + Plastics
- Glass + Food
- Glass + Garden
- Plastics + Food
- Plastics + Garden
- Food + Garden

Optional hint: Add the two building costs for each pair. A total equal to the budget is allowed.

### Stage 3 — Apply the environmental target

The selected pair must save **at least 40,000 tonnes CO₂e each year**.

| Facility | Estimated annual emissions saving |
| --- | ---: |
| Glass | 6,000 tonnes CO₂e |
| Plastics | 27,000 tonnes CO₂e |
| Food | 21,000 tonnes CO₂e |
| Garden | 10,000 tonnes CO₂e |

Savings compare Glass, Food and Garden with landfill, and Plastics with energy-generating incineration. They use expected waste quantities. Add the separate streams’ savings.

**Which pair meets both the building budget and the emissions target?**

Optional hint: Keep the budget restriction when applying the new target.

### Evaluation — choose one answer

**Which statement best justifies your decision?**

A. The cheaper affordable pair is preferable because lower building costs mean lower environmental impact.

B. The selected pair meets both targets using the forecast waste supply; its actual performance should still be monitored.

C. Meeting the emissions target establishes that the selected pair is the most sustainable option in every respect.

## Draft 2: Which recycling plan? — 12–15 minutes

Choose one plan within **£25m building plus five-year costs** and saving **at least 20,000 tonnes CO₂e/year** against the stated landfill baseline. If several qualify, choose the lowest five-year cost. Existing land and collection services are available; use fixed prices and a calculator.

A and B build food-digestion plants. C builds a smaller food plant and buys specialist treatment for non-reusable small electrical items. The latter is an operating service from an existing processor, not a new electrical facility.

### Stage 1 — Deduce costs with two linked grids

| Plan | Food capacity (tonnes/year) | Electrical service |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| A · Medium food | 30,000 | No |
| B · Large food | 45,000 | No |
| C · Small food + electrical | 20,000 | Yes |

Complete two three-by-three grids: plan against building costs **£6m/£12m/£20m**, and plan against annual costs **£0.5m/£1.4m/£1.7m**. Each value is used once in its grid. Annual figures include food operation and C's electrical service; do not print C's cost breakdown before marking.

1. A does not have the lowest building cost.
2. A costs less to build than B.
3. The plan with the lowest building cost has the highest annual cost.
4. Exactly one statement is true: B costs £20m to build; B costs £0.5m a year.

The clues combine an exclusion, relative ordering, a cross-category link and an exclusive alternative. Independently enumerating all 36 mappings gives one solution; removing each clue leaves 3, 2, 4 and 2 solutions respectively. Use the completed grids to select every plan within the £25m building-plus-five-year budget. Optional hint: combine the first two clues, then connect the grids and apply exactly one in the final clue.

### Stage 2 — Decide which evidence to use

The survey predicts 36,000 tonnes of suitable food waste. Checked delivery transactions for the latest full year record 26,000 tonnes, including 2,000 rejected. Entries include date, weight and acceptance; duplicates were removed and totals checked against weighbridge records. Collection arrangements and businesses served stay the same.

Three one-point questions retain the original CA6 practice: identify transactions as the source; choose checked actual deliveries over a full year as the cautious baseline; calculate suitable food supply. Distractors treat the larger forecast as automatically preferable or electronic records as error-free. The survey remains potentially useful for future engagement.

### Stage 3 — Compare environmental savings

Use the food supply calculated in Stage 2 and each plan's capacity. Food digestion saves **0.7 tonnes CO₂e per tonne**; electrical recycling saves **1.7 tonnes CO₂e per tonne**, both against landfill. Add separate streams' benefits.

A separate checked record identifies **5,000 tonnes/year of suitable electrical waste that cannot be reused**. Only C reserves specialist capacity for all of it. Calculate food processed and total emissions savings for A and C, then choose from all three plans. The source disclosure states that the electrical estimate assumes a material mix comparable to the published benchmark and suitable recycling outlets.

### Evaluation

Choose the statement supporting the plan that meets both targets using checked quantities, with continued monitoring, prevention and appropriate reuse. Distractors claim spare plant capacity guarantees greater savings, or that a higher per-tonne electrical benefit makes recycling universally preferable. The correct response concerns a bounded planning decision, not a universal material ranking.

## Teacher answers and marking proposal

Keep this section outside the student task until review. These are puzzle points, not a claim of official exam marks or automatic Core progress attribution.

### Starter

- Stage 1: Plastics £8m; Garden £9m; Glass £12m; Food £16m. Each comparison is necessary to establish this unique order. Award three points for the complete correct mapping.
- Stage 2: Glass + Plastics £20m; Glass + Garden £21m; Plastics + Food £24m; Plastics + Garden £17m qualify. Glass + Food £28m and Food + Garden £25m do not. Award one point for exactly the four affordable pairs.
- Stage 3: affordable pairs save 33,000, 16,000, 48,000 and 37,000 tonnes CO₂e/year respectively. Only Plastics + Food meets 40,000: one point.
- Evaluation: B, one point. A wrongly treats capital cost as an environmental measure. C claims a wider conclusion than the evidence supports.

Proposed total: six points. Timing allocation: cost grid 1½ minutes, budget 1 minute, environmental constraint 1½ minutes, evaluation and reading allowance 2 minutes. This is a trial target, not a measured result.

### Longer activity

- Stage 1: A builds for £12m and runs for £0.5m/year; B £20m and £1.4m/year; C £6m and £1.7m/year. Award three points for both complete correct grids. Five-year totals are A £14.5m, B £27m and C £14.5m; one point for exactly A/C. The first two clues fix the building costs, the third fixes C’s annual cost, and the exclusive alternative fixes B’s annual cost.
- Stage 2: Transactions; checked actual deliveries over a complete year with unchanged arrangements; 26,000 − 2,000 = 24,000 tonnes suitable food. One point each.
- Stage 3: A processes 24,000 tonnes food and saves 16,800 tonnes CO₂e. C processes 20,000 tonnes food, saving 14,000, plus 5,000 × 1.7 = 8,500 from electricals: 22,500 in total. One point per complete row and one for choosing C. B exceeds the budget and would still be limited by available food.
- Evaluation: B, one point. Meet both requirements, monitor outcomes and retain prevention/reuse priorities.

Total: eleven points. Target timing remains 12–15 minutes: linked grids and budget 4–5 minutes, evidence 3, combined benefits 3–4, evaluation/checking 2–3. The grids replace three cost-entry parts, keeping the total at eleven points. Actual mixed-ability completion time is unverified.

## Numerical provenance and limitations

The machine-readable [figure ledger](../references/esd/draft-figures.json) is the complete value-to-source map. Source facts, calculations and scenario assumptions are separate. Preserve this map when writing the eventual question records; do not replace it with a generic sustainability bibliography.

### Starter costs and annual effects

Capital benchmarks: glass £10m (2018), plastics £7m (plant opened 2021), food £12m (2015). ONS annual CPI values are 105.9, 111.6 and 100 respectively; 2024 is 133.9. Conversion is `reported cost × 133.9 / source-year index`. Results are approximately £12.64m, £8.40m and £16.07m. Rounding to £4m steps gives the draft's £12m, £8m and £16m; each change is under 6% of its converted value. This preserves a broad real-world scale, not a reliable procurement estimate. Project scope differs and CPI is only a general price-level proxy.

The glass case is a 60,000-tonne/year processing benchmark. Plastics' authored 20,000-tonne capacity approximates the reported 70 tonnes/day over 285 operating days. Food's benchmark is 45,000 tonnes/year. The scenario uses only 20,000, 10,000 and 30,000 tonnes/year respectively from the same fictional catchment; full capacity is not assumed. About 500,000 residents plus businesses is an authored scale assumption, not a measured local waste forecast.

Defra's Table 1 gives kg CO₂e per tonne managed. Differences between the chosen baseline and treatment are: glass `9 − (−326) = 335`; HDPE `2241 − (−485) = 2726`; food `627 − (−78) = 705`. Classroom rates are 0.3, 2.7 and 0.7 tonnes CO₂e per tonne respectively. Multiplying by the scenario flows gives 6,000, 27,000 and 21,000. Do not generalise these rankings to other disposal routes or plastics. In particular, food diversion from energy recovery would have a much smaller benefit than the landfill comparison used here.

The plastics scenario assumes HDPE displaces virgin HDPE; the glass scenario assumes container remelt. The commercial sources establish capital/process scale only, not those exact environmental outcomes. The Defra model supplies the treatment assumptions. Average process and transport burdens are already included. Do not add another credit for energy saved or biogas generated. Building emissions, full product lifecycles and site-specific transport are not assessed. The drafts use emissions as the single numerical environmental measure so energy is not added as another arithmetic task.

Garden uses Wastewise's 26 May 2020 report of a £7m Crewe composting facility with 75,000 tonnes/year capacity. CPI 2020 is 108.7: `7 × 133.9 / 108.7 = £8.624m`, rounded to £9m (under 5% change). The real plant processes mixed food and garden waste; the scenario allocates it 20,000 tonnes/year of separate garden waste, avoiding overlap with Food. Defra's garden factors are landfill 579 and composting 72 kg CO₂e/t; `(579 − 72) / 1000 = 0.507`, rounded to 0.5. The scenario saving is therefore 10,000 tonnes CO₂e/year.

Use the authored rounded figures to solve the task. The budget deliberately includes equality: Plastics + Food totals £24m. Its unrounded cost benchmarks total about £24.47m; these are not contingency-equipped procurement budgets. A 10% increase in estimated savings would also make Plastics + Garden qualify. The single classroom solution is not a claim of a robust investment decision under real uncertainty.

### Longer cost derivation

Use `2010 benchmark × reference electrical capacity × 133.9 / 89.4`. Cost units in the DECC tables are £thousand/MW, or £thousand/MW/year. Divide by 1,000 for £million. Round capital to £2m steps and annual running costs to £0.1m.

| Design | Reference capacity | Capital input | Operating input | Converted capital / annual running | Draft capital / annual running |
| --- | ---: | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Small (C) | 0.9 MW | Table 71, <1 MW median: 4,463 | Table 75, <1 MW median: 900 | £6.02m / £1.21m | £6m / £1.2m |
| Medium (A) | 1.5 MW | Table 74, food median: 5,241 | Table 77, food low: 219 | £11.77m / £0.49m | £12m / £0.5m |
| Large (B) | 2.0 MW | Table 74, food high: 6,915 | Table 77, food median: 481 | £20.71m / £1.44m | £20m / £1.4m |

These combine independent benchmark inputs to construct fictional alternatives. They do not describe measured paired costs of three real plants. The source supports variable operating costs and the effect of automation; it does not prove that a particular £12m plant will achieve £0.5m annual running costs. The 20/30/45-thousand-tonne capacities and their electrical-size pairings are plausible authored scale assumptions, not manufacturer specifications. Their uncertainty and the age of the benchmarks remain material limitations for publication review.

Food running allowances are fixed gross budgets over the exercise's flow range, including operation and maintenance. They are not gate fees or costs per delivered tonne. Do not count capital repayment again inside them. Income and feedstock purchases are excluded, following the scope of the source benchmarks. Existing land/collection arrangements are assumed; this is a bounded investment comparison, not a complete business case.

The 36,000-tonne survey, 26,000-tonne deliveries with 2,000 rejected (24,000 suitable), five-year period, £25m budget and 20,000-tonne target are authored constraints. No external source is represented as having measured them. The lower food quantity is an alternative scenario input, not a contradiction of the starter's 30,000-tonne planning case.

### Electrical service and environmental derivation

Defra's 2023 WEEE impact assessment Table 9 uses £75/tonne in 2019 prices for net small-mixed-WEEE treatment. This includes transport from collection points, containers, treatment and residual/hazardous handling, with primary material revenue already included. It excludes initial collection. ONS CPI 2019=107.8 gives `75 × 133.9 / 107.8 × 5,000 / 1,000,000 = £0.465793m/year`, rounded to £0.5m. It is a purchased service: no electrical construction cost or further material-revenue credit is added. Food allowances remain gross with no food income assumed; the common comparison is partnership budget outlay.

Table 77's consumption-based weighted electrical factors are landfill 9 and closed-loop recycling −1,665 kg CO₂e/tonne. The avoided impact is 1.674 tonnes/tonne, rounded to 1.7. At the authored 5,000-tonne flow, benefit is 8,500 tonnes/year. Its 55% steel, 12% aluminium, 7% glass and 26% plastic composition is a modelling assumption, not a measured Harbour City audit. Do not substitute the territorial factors in Table 16 or apply this estimate to every electrical item or reuse route. The fictional supply is assessed for reuse first; suitable processing capacity and outlets are assumed.

The existing processor approach avoids presenting service fees as new-plant operating costs. Investment announcements and an Italian plant model were investigated but not used for puzzle figures. The actual numerical sources are those linked above and recorded in the ledger.
